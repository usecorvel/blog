When I ran scheduling for a nine-location physical therapy group, we tracked cancellations as a
single number: visits lost per week. It was always somewhere between 30 and 45 across the group,
and it always felt like weather. Something that happened to us.

It took me an embarrassingly long time to work out what each one actually cost.

## The visit itself

A typical follow-up visit at that group billed around $120 after insurance adjustments. That's the
number everybody quotes, and it's the smallest part of the cost.

## The slot that doesn't come back

Late cancellations rarely refill on their own. In Corvel's data from clinics before they turn on
waitlist fill, only 18% of slots cancelled with less than 24 hours' notice were rebooked by anyone.
The other 82% stayed empty. A clinician on salary still works that hour, just without a patient in
front of them.

## The phone time

Most morning cancellations arrive as a text or a voicemail between 6:30 and 8:00. Someone at the
front desk has to read it, find the appointment, cancel it, try to rebook the patient, and then
(if they have time) call around to fill the gap. We timed this once. It averaged just over seven
minutes per cancellation, and the calls to fill the gap rarely worked because by the time anyone
made them it was already 9 a.m.

## Adding it up

For a clinic seeing 25 late cancellations a month, the rough maths looks like this:

| | Per cancellation | Per month |
|---|---|---|
| Lost visit revenue | $120 | $3,000 |
| Clinician time with no patient | $55 | $1,375 |
| Front-desk time | $4 | $100 |
| Plan-of-care visits that slip and never happen | ~$60 | ~$1,500 |
| **Total** | **~$239** | **~$5,975** |

The last row is the one that surprised me. A patient who cancels visit six of a twelve-visit plan
and doesn't get rebooked within a week is much less likely to finish the plan. That shows up months
later as a discharge that never happened.

## What actually helps

Three things moved the number for us, in order of impact:

1. **Answering the cancellation immediately with a new time.** Patients who get a reply with two
   openings while they're still holding their phone rebook about half the time. Patients who get a
   call back at 10 a.m. rebook far less.
2. **Offering the slot to someone who wants it.** A waitlist of patients who asked for an earlier
   visit turns an empty hour into a filled one, if you reach them fast enough.
3. **Making the policy clear before, not after.** The reminder text two days out is where the
   cancellation policy belongs.

None of this is complicated. It just needs to happen at 7 a.m., which is the problem.
